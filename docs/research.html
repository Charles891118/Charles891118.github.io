The Impact of Putative Income Regulations on Social Assistance: A Policy Simulation Study Using a Model Family Approach and Administrative Data

Julia Shu-Huah Wang, Kuan-Ming Chen, Chia-Le Yen, Wan-Hsuan Chiang, Pin-Chen Chiang, Jing Wu, Chien-Yu Lin, Pang-Hau Wu, John Jih-Chang Liang, Fang-Ling Chang, Yueh-Tzu Wang, Jia-Wei Shen, Chu-Kai Cheng, and Xiao-Yi Yu. Journal of Social Sciences and Philosophy (2026). Advance online publication. [Article in Chinese]

Read More

Taiwan's social assistance system emphasizes work ethics and self-reliance. A key feature is the "putative income" regulation, which imputes earnings to non-working applicants. This policy has been controversial, as many genuinely struggling households are deemed ineligible for assistance. While past research has used interviews to critique the system's fairness, this study employs model family data and government administrative records to simulate the effects of abolishing the regulation on benefit generosity, poverty reduction, and coverage. Results show that removing putative income would significantly increase the income of zero-earning households, helping them meet basic needs. However, it would not substantially expand social assistance coverage. International comparisons indicate that abolishing the regulation would not transform Taiwan into a generous welfare state. Reforming the current benefit calculation to better reflect the needs of low-wage earners could enhance fairness and adequacy in supporting families in economic distress.

Keywords: social assistance, putative income, work ethics, model family, policy simulation

Examining the longitudinal influence of loneliness on healthcare utilization: evidence from Taiwan’s national health insurance data

Shiau-Fang Chao, Hui-Chuan Hsu, Chia-Le Yen and Bo-Yu Chen. BMC Geriatrics, 25(1), Article 410 (2025).

Read More

Background: This study combines a nationally representative sample from Taiwan with four years of National Health Insurance (NHI) data to explore the distinctive impact of emotional and social loneliness on health service utilization, including outpatient visits for mental health, general outpatient visits, emergency room (ER) visits, and hospitalization.

Methods: Data were drawn from the 2015 Taiwan Longitudinal Survey on Aging (TLSA) and merged with participants’ NHI records from 2015 to 2018. The analysis used logistic regression for binary outcomes and negative binomial regression for counts.

Results: Results show that higher emotional loneliness in 2015 was associated with increased outpatient mental health visits over time and more general outpatient visits within the same year. Conversely, higher social loneliness in 2015 reduced the likelihood of seeking ER care in 2015.

Conclusions: By merging national data and distinguishing emotional from social loneliness, this study offers insights into their differential impacts on healthcare utilization among older adults in Taiwan. It emphasizes the importance of addressing loneliness to improve physical and mental well-being and optimize the effective utilization of healthcare resources.

Keywords: Emotional loneliness, Social loneliness, Health service utilization, Longitudinal, Taiwan

Comparing Four Frailty Index Construction Methods for Mortality Prediction in the Health and Retirement Study

Chia-Le Yen, Yu-Chih Chen, You-Guang Yang, and Jessica Fang.

Poster presentation, Society for Social Work and Research 31st Annual Conference, San Francisco, CA, January 2027.

Read More

Background and Objectives: The frailty index of cumulative deficits (FI-CD) is widely used to quantify frailty as the proportion of observed health deficits, yet its equal-weighting assumption has been criticized for treating all deficits as interchangeable. Principal component analysis (PCA) and item response theory (IRT) have emerged as leading alternatives for constructing empirically weighted indices. However, existing comparisons have focused primarily on predictive performance, with limited attention to score concordance across demographic subgroups or consistency in identifying the most informative deficits. This study systematically compared four frailty indices—two cumulative-deficit versions (FI-CD60, FI-CD35), FI-PCA, and FI-IRT—on inter-method agreement, subgroup invariance, item-level consistency, and predictive validity for all-cause mortality.

Methods: Using data from the Health and Retirement Study (1998–2022), we conducted a longitudinal analysis of adults aged 50 and older. 60 health deficit variables were retained after excluding items with more than 5% missing data and those too rare (<1%) or too common (>80%) to differentiate frailty levels. To avoid redundancy among conceptually overlapping items, those sharing similar content were combined into ordinal scales, yielding 35 analytic items. Missing covariates were addressed via listwise deletion; missing deficit values were handled through multiple imputation by chained equations (m = 5), with FI-CD35, FI-PCA, and FI-IRT scores averaged across imputed datasets. The final sample comprised 192,578 person-wave observations from 35,403 individuals. FI-CD60 and FI-CD35 were calculated as mean deficit proportions, FI-PCA was derived from polychoric PCA retaining 5 components identified by parallel analysis, and FI-IRT was estimated using graded response models with scores rescaled to 0–1. Inter-method concordance was assessed using Pearson and Spearman correlations, computed overall and by sex, age, and ethnicity. Predictive validity for all-cause mortality was evaluated with survey-weighted discrete-time complementary log–log models, adjusting for sex, age, ethnicity, education, smoking, and alcohol use.

Results: FI-CD60 (mean = 0.168, SD = 0.128), FI-CD35 (mean = 0.203, SD = 0.149), and FI-PCA (mean = 0.179, SD = 0.153) displayed positively skewed distributions, whereas FI-IRT (mean = 0.477, SD = 0.151) approximated a bell-shaped distribution. All four indices showed high concordance: Pearson correlations ranged from 0.921 to 0.985 and Spearman correlations from 0.958 to 0.990, with comparable magnitudes across subgroups (Pearson: 0.915–0.987; Spearman: 0.949–0.993). PCA and IRT exhibited strong item-level consistency, identifying the same top 4 most informative deficits: bathing difficulty, housework limitation, impairment-related limitation, and walking difficulty. Adjusted mortality models yielded near-equivalent predictive accuracy: pseudo-R² values were 0.217 for FI-CD60, 0.215 for FI-CD35, 0.216 for FI-PCA, and 0.218 for FI-IRT.

Conclusions and Implications: The four frailty indices produced near-equivalent mortality predictive accuracy and high inter-method concordance across demographic subgroups, suggesting that cumulative-deficit scores remain strong benchmarks for aging research. Although FI-CD performs well for risk stratification and service planning, researchers seeking to understand which deficits contribute most to frailty severity may benefit from applying PCA or IRT, as both methods consistently identified functional dependency items as carrying the greatest weight. These findings advance methodological rigor in health measurement and clarify when added modeling complexity is warranted.

Old-age Social Assistance and Household Responses: A Triple-Difference Analysis from Taiwan

Chia-Le Yen, Kuan-Ming Chen, and Julia Shu-Huah Wang.

Poster presentation, Society for Social Work and Research 31st Annual Conference, San Francisco, CA, January 2027.
Oral presentation, Association for Public Policy Analysis and Management 48th Annual Fall Research Conference, Boston, MA, November 2026.

Read More

This study examines how Taiwan’s means-tested social assistance affects the way near-poor families provide care, work, and housing for an older adult. Eligibility is assessed at the household level, and because working-age adults without earnings are assigned imputed income at the minimum wage, an imputation that stops at age 65, a household becomes much more likely to qualify once a member turns 65, even though its actual resources are unchanged. Using this jump in eligibility at 65, we compare age-65 with age-60 cohorts, and households that just pass the means test with near-poor households that just fail it, in a triple-difference design applied to individual-level administrative data for 2013 to 2022.

The results show that crossing the age-65 threshold raises the probability of receiving assistance by roughly 2.5 to 5.3 percentage points, and this eligibility gain clearly changes family behavior. Older adults become more likely to enter long-term care and to move, so assistance helps support formal care rather than keeping them in place. Spouses and adult children work less, with employment falling by about 2 to 3 percentage points and spouse earnings declining; adult children also co-reside with the parent less and are less likely to stay in the same county, stepping back from care and proximity. At the same time, the children’s own marriage and childbearing rise, more so for sons, consistent with sons carrying more of the traditional duty to support aging parents. Overall, social assistance substitutes for the care, income, and housing support families had provided, and by easing children’s support burden it frees them to start families of their own, so its effects reach well beyond the recipient and reallocate responsibility between the state and the family.

Keywords: Old-age social assistance; Living arrangements; Family labor supply; Intergenerational support; Triple-difference design

Frailty and Mortality Among Older Americans: A 20-Year Follow-up from the Health and Retirement Study

Yu-Chih Chen, Chia-Le Yen, Jessica Fang, and You-Guang Yang.

Poster presentation, Society for Social Work and Research 31st Annual Conference, San Francisco, CA, January 2027.

Read More

Background and Purpose: Approximately 9% to 34% of adults in the US are considered frail, with prevalence rising significantly with age. Frailty is central to geriatric social work and healthcare as it represents cumulative physiological and functional vulnerabilities associated with elevated risks of hospitalization, institutionalization, and adverse health outcomes such as multimorbidity and mortality. Understanding frailty and its health impacts thus has implications for preventing premature death and extending healthy life expectancy. However, the current evidence remains mixed due to inconsistent variable selection, varied frailty model construction, diverse age-sampling without focusing on older adults, and inconsistent mortality outcomes. To address these gaps, this study constructs the frailty index using the standardized cumulative deficit model and a model-based principal component analysis served as a sensitivity test, and investigates their predictive power for mortality among adults aged 50+ using a US population-based panel survey.

Methods: We used the 1998-2022 RAND Health and Retirement Study (HRS) longitudinal file and selected participants aged 50+ across all waves, yielding 35,403 respondents and 192,578 person-waves. Frailty was constructed using 35 variables (e.g., health status, chronic conditions, mobility, ADLs, IADLs, sensory impairment, etc.). Two mortality outcomes, all-cause and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality, were selected from the HRS Tracker file. Two approaches were used to construct the frailty indices: a standardized method that combined all 35 variables into a continuous cumulative deficit score (FI-CD; range: 0-1) and a PCA-based analysis to reduce the multidimensionality of frailty (FI-PCA); missing values in frailty were handled using multiple imputation (n=5). We used a lagged design to model the influence of frailty (t-1) on mortality (t), controlling for age, gender, race, education, smoking, alcohol use, and wave-time, using discrete-time survival analysis with robust standard errors that account for the complex survey design. For easier interpretation, frailty was divided by 100, making the percentage changes in mortality correspond to a 0.01-unit change in frailty.

Results: A strong correlation (Pearson r=0.984; Spearman ρ=0.990) was observed between FI-CD and FI-PCA. Overall, both frailty measures showed similar distributions (Mean/SD: FI-CD [0.203/0.149]; FI-PCA [0.179/0.153]), and they increase with age over time. The discrete-time survival analysis showed that frailty was significantly associated with 4.0-4.7% increases in all-cause mortality (FI-CD HRs: 1.047; FI-PCA HRs: 1.040) and 3.8-4.6% increases in CVD mortality (FI-CD HRs: 1.046; FI-PCA HRs: 1.038).

Conclusions and Implications: Findings suggest that frailty is predictive of and positively associated with both all-cause and CVD mortality. Our findings have implications for one of the Grand Challenges in Social Work: closing health gaps, particularly through improved frailty measurement and timely health interventions. As frailty reflects a risk continuum and a potentially reversible process that can be modified, we urge further research using varied algorithms to develop an accurate measurement protocol for frailty assessment and detection. Additionally, since frailty integrates multilevel declines in physiological and functional health, health-promoting actions (e.g., healthy diet, exercise and rehabilitation, health monitoring, social care services, and aging-friendly community programs) should be implemented to reduce age-related declines in health and support healthy longevity across the life course.
