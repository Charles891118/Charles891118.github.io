The Impact of Putative Income Regulations on Social Assistance: A Policy Simulation Study Using a Model Family Approach and Administrative Data

Julia Shu-Huah Wang, Kuan-Ming Chen, Chia-Le Yen, Wan-Hsuan Chiang, Pin-Chen Chiang, Jing Wu, Chien-Yu Lin, Pang-Hau Wu, John Jih-Chang Liang, Fang-Ling Chang, Yueh-Tzu Wang, Jia-Wei Shen, Chu-Kai Cheng, and Xiao-Yi Yu. Journal of Social Sciences and Philosophy (In Press).

Read More

Taiwan's social assistance system emphasizes work ethics and self-reliance. A key feature is the "putative income" regulation, which imputes earnings to non-working applicants. This policy has been controversial, as many genuinely struggling households are deemed ineligible for assistance. While past research has used interviews to critique the system's fairness, this study employs model family data and government administrative records to simulate the effects of abolishing the regulation on benefit generosity, poverty reduction, and coverage. Results show that removing putative income would significantly increase the income of zero-earning households, helping them meet basic needs. However, it would not substantially expand social assistance coverage. International comparisons indicate that abolishing the regulation would not transform Taiwan into a generous welfare state. Reforming the current benefit calculation to better reflect the needs of low-wage earners could enhance fairness and adequacy in supporting families in economic distress.

Keywords: social assistance, putative income, work ethics, model family, policy simulation

Examining the longitudinal influence of loneliness on healthcare utilization: evidence from Taiwan’s national health insurance data

Shiau-Fang Chao, Hui-Chuan Hsu, Chia-Le Yen and Bo-Yu Chen. BMC Geriatrics (2025).

Read More

Background: This study combines a nationally representative sample from Taiwan with four years of National Health Insurance (NHI) data to explore the distinctive impact of emotional and social loneliness on health service utilization, including outpatient visits for mental health, general outpatient visits, emergency room (ER) visits, and hospitalization.

Methods: Data were drawn from the 2015 Taiwan Longitudinal Survey on Aging (TLSA) and merged with participants’ NHI records from 2015 to 2018. The analysis used logistic regression for binary outcomes and negative binomial regression for counts.

Results: Results show that higher emotional loneliness in 2015 was associated with increased outpatient mental health visits over time and more general outpatient visits within the same year. Conversely, higher social loneliness in 2015 reduced the likelihood of seeking ER care in 2015.

Conclusions: By merging national data and distinguishing emotional from social loneliness, this study offers insights into their differential impacts on healthcare utilization among older adults in Taiwan. It emphasizes the importance of addressing loneliness to improve physical and mental well-being and optimize the effective utilization of healthcare resources.

Keywords: Emotional loneliness, Social loneliness, Health service utilization, Longitudinal, Taiwan

Old-age Social Assistance and Household Responses: A Triple-Difference Analysis from Taiwan

Coauthored with Kuan-Ming Chen and Julia Shu-Huah Wang

Read More

This study examines how Taiwan’s means-tested social assistance affects the way near-poor families provide care, work, and housing for an older adult. Eligibility is assessed at the household level, and because working-age adults without earnings are assigned imputed income at the minimum wage, an imputation that stops at age 65, a household becomes much more likely to qualify once a member turns 65, even though its actual resources are unchanged. Using this jump in eligibility at 65, we compare age-65 with age-60 cohorts, and households that just pass the means test with near-poor households that just fail it, in a triple-difference design applied to individual-level administrative data for 2013 to 2022.

The results show that crossing the age-65 threshold raises the probability of receiving assistance by roughly 2.5 to 5.3 percentage points, and this eligibility gain clearly changes family behavior. Older adults become more likely to enter long-term care and to move, so assistance helps support formal care rather than keeping them in place. Spouses and adult children work less, with employment falling by about 2 to 3 percentage points and spouse earnings declining; adult children also co-reside with the parent less and are less likely to stay in the same county, stepping back from care and proximity. At the same time, the children’s own marriage and childbearing rise, more so for sons, consistent with sons carrying more of the traditional duty to support aging parents. Overall, social assistance substitutes for the care, income, and housing support families had provided, and by easing children’s support burden it frees them to start families of their own, so its effects reach well beyond the recipient and reallocate responsibility between the state and the family.

Keywords: Old-age social assistance; Living arrangements; Family labor supply; Intergenerational support; Triple-difference design
